01 Starting from scratch

Draw the feature before the frame, in broad strokes, and stop when the screen can ship.

Start with a feature, not a layout

Draw the fields and the one action someone came for before you place a logo, a nav, or a footer. The frame can wait until the feature has a shape.

Detail comes later

Early screens only need boxes, weight, and space. Icons, shadows, and a typeface are how you finish a decision, not how you find one. Stay in grey until the order of the page is obvious.

Don’t design too much

If you cannot ship attachments, rewards, or sharing this week, leave them off the screen. A control that does nothing teaches people the product is unfinished.

Choose a personality

Font, color, corner radius, and the voice of the button are the personality. Pick one family and keep every control in it. Square and fully round do not belong on the same card.

Limit your choices

A short scale with obvious steps beats a picker full of near-duplicates. Decide the steps once, then choose the neighbor that is clearly bigger or smaller. If two sizes feel the same, drop one.

02 Hierarchy is everything

Rank what people should see first. Weight and color do more of that work than raw size.

Not all elements are equal

A screen where every number wears the same weight reads as a wall. Give one figure the lead, and let the others sit back as support.

Size isn’t everything

A huge title and a tiny subtitle make the page feel thin and hard to read. Two weights, around 400 and 700, plus a darker and a quieter color, carry most UI hierarchy without a size jump.

Don’t use grey text on colored backgrounds

Grey on a green fill looks muddy because the hue clashes, not because the words are secondary. Keep the background’s hue and lift the lightness until the line is quiet but still readable.

Emphasize by de-emphasizing

When the current item still will not stand out, quiet the competitors. Lighter color and a normal weight on the rest does more than another loud color on the one you care about.

Labels are a last resort

An email, a phone number, and a price usually explain themselves. Drop the label when the format is enough, and let the name carry more weight than the role under it.

Separate visual hierarchy from document hierarchy

A heading can be a small label. The outline decides which element is the section title. Size, weight, and color decide what a person reads first.

Balance weight and contrast

A heavy icon in pure black outweighs the word beside it. Soften the icon, or thicken a light border, until the two carry similar visual weight. Contrast and area trade off.

Semantics are secondary

One solid primary, a quieter secondary, and text for the rest. Destructive red belongs on the action when that action is the point, as in a confirm step, not on every screen that happens to contain it.

03 Layout and spacing

Start with more space than you need, then pull every gap from one scale.

Start with too much white space

Cramped layouts rarely loosen themselves. Begin past the comfortable amount, then drag space back until the group feels held together. Dense UI is a choice you make on purpose.

Establish a spacing and sizing system

Pick a scale and stop inventing pixels. Steps should be far enough apart that the next one is an obvious choice, not a one-pixel argument. A useful set: 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64.

You don’t have to fill the whole screen

Give a form the width it needs and leave the rest empty. A wide monitor is not a reason to stretch a two-field search across the desk. Shrink the canvas when a small UI feels lost.

Grids are overrated

A sidebar wants a fixed width, not a percentage that grows with the monitor and collapses on a phone. Let the main column flex. Use a grid when the content is actually a grid.

Relative sizing doesn’t scale

Padding tied to the font size balloons when the heading grows. Choose type from the type scale and spacing from the spacing scale. They are allowed to change on their own.

Avoid ambiguous spacing

Space inside a group stays smaller than space between groups. Equal gaps make unrelated lines look like one list. Turn the gaps on if you want the numbers.

04 Designing text

A type scale, a comfortable measure, and alignment that follows how the line is read.

Establish a type scale

Hand-pick a handful of sizes and ignore the ones in between. Nested ems drift off the scale. Use px or rem from the list: 12, 14, 16, 18, 20, 24, 30, 36.

Use good fonts

UI text wants a face with several weights and a comfortable body size. A mono or display face can label a chart. It should not carry the paragraph. Stay at 400 or heavier for interface copy.

Keep your line length in check

Body lines sit best around 45 to 75 characters. On a wide column, cap the paragraph even if the card beside it is wider. The slider starts in that range. Push it and the return sweep gets harder to find.

Baseline, not center

Mixed sizes in one line should share a baseline. Vertical centering makes the small currency mark float beside the price instead of sitting on the same line.

Line-height is proportional

Large headlines can sit near a line-height of 1. Long, smaller text needs more air, closer to 1.5 or above. One leading for both makes the title float and the paragraph feel off.

Not every link needs a color

A primary link can be the body color at a heavier weight. Ancillary links can wait for hover. Saving blue for the actions that are actually links keeps the paragraph from turning into a map of underlines.

Align with readability in mind

Left-align anything long enough to wrap. Center a short title if you want, not a paragraph. In a table, text stays left and numbers sit on the right so the digits line up.

Use letter-spacing effectively

Tighten tracking on a large headline set in a body face. Open it on a short all-caps label. The same spacing on both makes the title feel stretched and the label feel cramped.

05 Working with color

Build ramps you can name. Add color after hierarchy already works in grey.

Ditch hex for HSL

Hue, saturation, and lightness are the knobs you actually turn. Hex changes all three at once, so a lighter green is a guess. Move one slider and read both notations.

hsl(160 54% 28%) #216e54

You need more colors than you think

Five swatches run out on the second screen. You want a run of greys, a run of the brand, and small ramps for success, warning, and danger. Most of the UI is the grey ramp.

Define your shades up front

Lightening a hex by hand produces three greens nobody can tell apart. Name the stops first, then assign the button, the badge, and the text from that ramp. Do not mint a new green for a hover.

Don’t let lightness kill your saturation

A tint at the same saturation as the middle stop turns chalky, and a shade turns muddy. As lightness moves away from the middle, raise saturation a little so the ramp still looks like the same color.

Greys don’t have to be grey

Pure grey feels sterile next to a warm page or a cool brand. Tint the whole ramp a few degrees toward blue or toward amber, and use that tint everywhere so the neutrals belong to the product.

Accessible doesn’t have to mean ugly

White text on a near-black green passes contrast and still feels heavy. Flip it: dark text on a light tint of the same hue, and keep the solid color for the button. You meet the ratio without painting the whole card in the darkest stop.

Don’t rely on color alone

Red and green are not a status system if the words or icons are missing. Pair the hue with a label and a mark so the state still reads in grey, and for people who do not see that hue difference.

06 Creating depth

Light comes from above. Shadows mean elevation, and flat UI can still be layered.

Emulate a light source

Treat the top edge as the lit one. A raised control gets a lighter top and a shadow underneath. A highlight along the bottom reads as light from below the desk, which makes the control feel sunk or fake.

Use shadows to convey elevation

One dramatic shadow on every surface makes nothing feel closer. Keep about five levels: a tight shadow for buttons, a little more for cards, more again for menus, and the widest for a modal.

Shadows can have two parts

A single soft shadow looks like a blur stuck to the card. Pair a tight, darker shadow for the contact edge with a larger, lighter one for the ambient lift. At higher elevation, let the tight shadow fade.

Even flat designs can have depth

Without a blur you can still separate layers: a lighter surface on a darker page, or a solid offset shadow. Same-white on same-white needs a border to exist. The surface shift does that job.

Overlap elements to create layers

When two regions touch at an edge, they read as stacked boxes. Pull an avatar across that edge and the page becomes two layers. Overlap is depth without a shadow.

07 Working with images

Photos, icons, and uploads are layout. Treat their size and contrast as part of the design.

Use good photos

A flash and a tilted horizon will not be fixed by a filter later. Pick a frame with a clear subject and a simple background, or draw a simple scene. Do not plan to swap in a phone picture at the end.

Text needs consistent contrast

White type on the bright part of a photo fails as soon as the image changes. Put the words on a steady scrim, or in a band you control, so the contrast does not depend on one lucky crop.

Everything has an intended size

A 16-pixel icon blown up is a stack of blocks. A dense screenshot shrunk to a thumbnail is dust. Draw the icon for the size you show, and crop a screenshot to the part someone should actually read.

Beware user-uploaded content

People upload wide banners and tall crops. Give every image the same frame, cover the frame, and add a faint inner shadow so a white photo does not bleed into a white card.

08 Finishing touches

Polish the pieces people actually meet: defaults, empty screens, and the borders you can delete.

Supercharge the defaults

Bullets, checks, and quotes arrive looking like a document. Restyle the ones people see all the time so they belong to the product. Leave the rare ones alone.

Add color with accent borders

A full box in brand color is loud. A thick edge on the side, in the hue that matches the message, colors the row without repainting it. Use it on notes, alerts, and highlighted list items.

Decorate your backgrounds

A flat grey panel is a placeholder. A quiet dot grid, a tint, or a soft change in the page color gives the region a material. Keep the contrast low so the content stays in front.

Don’t overlook empty states

The first time someone opens a feature, the screen is empty. That view needs a sentence and the action that fills it. Hide filters and tabs that do nothing until there is something to filter.

Use fewer borders

A box around every row makes a cage. Separate with space, a background shift, or a single quiet edge. Add a border only when those are not enough.

Think outside the box

A stock dropdown is a column of labels. If each choice has a time and a price, show those on a selectable card. Combine what a person compares instead of hiding it in a menu.

09 Leveling up

Taste is a stack of reviewed decisions. Keep a short loop and the screens you ship get calmer without a new theory each week.

Copy one interface you admire and rebuild the hierarchy in grey before you open a color picker. When something will not pop, quiet a neighbor. When a spacing value is not on the scale, it does not ship. The checks below stay in this browser.
